Composition first

Plan the main subject and the space around it.

Japanese prints offer a useful place to research imagery and compositions you like. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston’s Tattoos in Japanese Prints exhibition connects tattoo imagery in early nineteenth-century Edo, now Tokyo, with color woodblock prints known as ukiyo-e. Its discussion of Utagawa Kuniyoshi’s work includes tattooed heroes decorated with snakes, peonies, supernatural figures, and dragons. If a print appeals to you, bring it as a reference and identify the artist or title when you can. Explain whether the subject, pose, surrounding pattern, or color is what you want to explore.

The portfolio photographs give you three specific compositions to study. The Buddha face occupies much of the back, with a flower below it and curling shapes around it. The snake’s body forms several loops, with blue underside areas, dark scales, yellow details, and pink-red flowers. The tiger appears among black and gray background forms and red leaves. Getty’s guide to design principles describes how lines, edges, shapes, and color can direct attention through an image. For your design, decide which subject or feature you want people to notice first and where it should sit on the body.

Design building blocks

Subjects, movement, and supporting elements.

Prepare a few priorities for the design discussion: the subject you want, its pose, the surrounding imagery, and the amount of color or shading you prefer. Use these four choices to explain your references and ask questions about the proposed tattoo.

01

Primary Subject

Choose the subject you most want to include, whether that is a tiger, snake, dragon, koi, warrior, or another figure. Bring references showing its face, body, or pose. If a particular character, story, or religious image matters, identify it clearly and explain the connection you want represented. Rank additional subjects so the main idea has a clear starting point.

02

Body Flow

Discuss how the proposed drawing would fit your chosen area. On an arm or leg, ask which parts would face outward and which would wrap around the limb. For a backpiece, review the position of the main subject and the intended edges. Ask to see the proposed placement with the nearby joint bent and extended, where relevant.

03

Background

Choose supporting imagery deliberately. Bring references for the waves, clouds, leaves, flowers, or other forms you want around the subject. Point out how much background coverage you prefer and where you want skin left visible. If a specific plant, season, or story matters to the design, name it and discuss how the elements should relate before settling on a layout.

04

Contrast & Color

Compare the blue, black, and yellow areas of the snake with the tiger’s red leaves and shaded background. Color choice also involves value, meaning how light or dark a color appears, as explained in Getty’s elements of art guide. Identify the palette you like and the areas you want lighter or darker. Discuss the main subject and background together.

Planning large work

Think beyond the first session.

Describe the full area you eventually want tattooed, even if you intend to begin with one section. Specify the upper and lower boundaries, the subjects you consider essential, and any existing tattoos in the area. Ask how the first section would relate to later work and which decisions need to be made before starting. Discuss appointment length, your availability, budget, and healing between sessions. Request an estimate and proposed sequence for your actual design, and ask how changes or additions could affect that plan.

  • Choose the body area and intended boundaries. Bring photos from several angles and approximate height and width measurements, including the space available around existing tattoos.
  • Identify the main subject, pose, and direction. Bring details of faces, scales, stripes, clothing, or other features you want included, and rank them by importance.
  • Discuss full color, selected color accents, or black-and-gray. Point out the palette and shading you like in your references, including any colors you prefer to avoid.
  • Show existing tattoos that the project needs to incorporate or work around. Explain which elements you want to keep, connect, rework, or leave visually separate.
  • Ask about the proposed number and length of appointments, healing intervals, and estimate. Share your availability and discuss how each stage would fit your schedule.

Japanese tattoo FAQ

Questions clients ask before starting.

Do I need to know the exact full design?+

Start with the main subject, preferred body area, approximate size, and references you can explain. Identify what you like in each image: a pose, face, palette, flower, or background pattern. For a larger project, bring the subjects you most want included and describe the intended boundaries. If you are undecided about a detail, make that part of the discussion. Ask which choices need to be settled before drawing or booking and which can be considered later in the proposed project.

Can Josh connect new work to an existing tattoo?+

Ask Josh to assess the existing work and available space. Bring clear, current photos of the full area from several angles, including nearby tattoos and the skin between them. Explain whether you want the new piece to connect, frame the old work, or remain separate. Point out any lines, colors, or subjects you want retained. If part of the project involves a cover-up or rework, include that in the initial discussion so it can be evaluated alongside the proposed new imagery.

Can a Japanese tattoo be black-and-gray?+

Discuss a black-and-gray version of your idea with Josh and bring references showing the shading you prefer. Identify the subject and background separately, including areas you would like lighter, darker, or left as visible skin. If your reference is a color print, explain which shapes and details matter most when considering a different palette. You can also ask about selected color accents. Review the proposed treatment at the intended size and alongside nearby tattoos before deciding on the palette for your project.

How long does a sleeve or backpiece take?+

Request an estimate for the design, size, and placement you agree on with Josh. Ask how long the proposed appointments would be, which areas or stages they would cover, and how healing between visits would affect the schedule. Share your availability and any planned events or travel you need to consider. Confirm the expected cost and what happens if the design changes. Before each appointment, ask for the preparation and aftercare instructions relevant to that session. Use the agreed project plan to organize your time and budget.
