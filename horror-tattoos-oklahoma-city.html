Mood through design

Light, shadow, and texture give the subject its form.

Start by identifying what you want someone to notice first: the eyes, a mask, an expression, or a creature’s silhouette. Getty’s guide to design principles explains that emphasis comes from differences in size, color, texture, and shape. Use that idea when discussing your tattoo: decide which feature deserves the most attention and which details can support it. A portrait, a full figure, and a scene with several characters call for different compositions. Choose the subject before adding a setting, lettering, or extra props.

The photographs show several ways to approach a horror subject. Freddy’s portrait includes warm facial colors, gray hat shading, and red-and-green clothing. Jason’s mask has pale yellow-brown areas, dark eye openings, and red markings. Pennywise has red hair and facial accents above a shaded, pleated collar. When comparing references, look at both the palette and the light-to-dark differences. Getty’s elements of art guide calls that lightness or darkness value and explains how line, color, and shading can depict texture. Ask how those choices might translate into the face, fabric, metal, or background in your design.

Building the image

Four decisions that shape a horror tattoo.

Use these four decisions to prepare for a consultation. A few clear priorities give Josh something specific to discuss, whether you want one recognizable portrait, an original monster, or the beginning of a larger horror collection.

01

Subject

Name the main subject and the details you consider essential. For a character, that might be a particular mask, expression, costume, or prop. For an original creature, describe its anatomy and surroundings. Decide whether you want a head, a full figure, or a scene. If several subjects matter, rank them so the design discussion has a starting point.

02

Tone

Describe the tone with examples. A grinning character portrait, an abandoned hallway, and a creature emerging from fog are three different directions to discuss. Say how much gore, distortion, humor, or realism you want. If a reference appeals to you because of its lighting or setting, point that out; liking the subject alone leaves those choices open.

03

Contrast

Look for light areas as well as dark ones in your references. Note where the face ends, where a hat or mask begins, and how much background surrounds the subject. Bring examples of the amount of shadow you like. Ask where the design could leave skin visible and which edges or small features need a clearer separation.

04

Placement

Bring approximate height and width measurements and show the area you have in mind. Discuss which parts of the image would face forward and which would wrap around the arm or leg. Mention nearby tattoos, planned additions, and whether you want the piece visible in everyday clothing. Ask to review the proposed size and position on your body.

Original or recognizable

Bring references for the version you want.

For a recognizable character, identify the film, adaptation, or version you prefer. Bring a clear face reference and any separate image needed to explain the costume or prop. A screenshot can help establish expression or lighting; mention if you want its crop changed or an object removed. For an original concept, label what you like in each reference, such as the creature’s jaw, the fabric, or the setting. Use other tattoos to explain a style preference, and bring source images that show the subject itself. These notes give the consultation a more precise brief.

  • Identify the main subject, its version, and the expression or pose you want. Separate the details you consider essential from ideas you are happy to change.
  • Choose references for the drawing style. Point out whether you prefer photographic detail, visible outlines, simplified shapes, or a combination, and explain which example comes closest.
  • Discuss black-and-gray, full color, or selected color accents. Name any colors tied to the character that you want included, along with colors you would prefer to avoid.
  • Show the proposed placement with approximate dimensions. Include nearby tattoos in your photos and say whether this is a standalone piece or part of a future sleeve.
  • Explain any content limits, such as gore, weapons, lettering, or religious imagery. If a date, title, or quotation matters, bring the exact wording and check its spelling.

Horror tattoo FAQ

Questions before the lights go out.

Can Josh tattoo a specific horror character?+

The portfolio examples on this page include Freddy, Jason Voorhees, and Pennywise. To discuss another character or a different version, bring clear references and specify the expression, costume, and props that matter to you. Ask about the proposed crop and which features would be included at your preferred size. If you want lettering or a film title, include that in the initial brief so it can be considered with the portrait. Confirm the design direction with Josh before treating a reference image as the final tattoo layout.

Is black-and-gray or color better for horror work?+

Choose by comparing the subject and the treatment you prefer. For black-and-gray, bring a reference that shows the light and shadow you want. For color, identify the costume, skin, or background colors that matter. The examples here give you concrete options to discuss: Freddy’s red-and-green sweater, Jason’s red mask markings, and Pennywise’s red hair and nose. You can also ask about a mostly black-and-gray design with selected color accents. Discuss the full composition, including the background and nearby tattoos, before settling on a palette.

How small can a detailed horror portrait be?+

Ask Josh to assess the particular portrait at the size and placement you want. Specify which features you expect to see, such as teeth, individual mask holes, facial creases, or a detailed glove. Request a comparison between a larger version and a simpler crop if the full reference includes more than you want to fit. A head alone and a face with hands, props, and background are different design requests. Review the proposed dimensions on your body and ask what details would be omitted or simplified at that size.

Can multiple horror subjects become a sleeve?+

Discuss the entire arm as a project, including any tattoos already there. Bring a list of characters and rank your favorites, then ask where the main portraits, smaller objects, and background could go. Decide whether you want separate pieces or subjects connected by a shared setting. Include the inner arm, elbow area, and wrist in the placement discussion, even if you intend to start with one section. Ask about the proposed order of work, appointment estimates, and how later additions would connect before booking the first part.
